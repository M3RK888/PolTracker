How this estimate works

Individual House races are rarely polled, so every district is estimated the same way: its partisan lean (Cook PVI) applied to the national environment from the generic congressional ballot, currently . A district rated R+5 is about 5 points more Republican than the country in two-party share, roughly 10 points of margin, so with the nation at D+8 it comes out near D-2.

Cook's 2026 PVI is calculated for the district lines actually used this year, so states that redrew their maps mid-decade (Texas, California, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, Utah and others) are covered.

This ignores candidates: incumbents, scandals and money often move a district several points from its lean. Ratings use the same cutoffs as the other maps. This view is a snapshot of the polls; switch to Forecast for chances of winning.

How the forecast works

This is an unvalidated hobby model. It uses a standard method, but its settings are judgment calls, and it hasn't been tested against past elections the way professional forecasts are. Treat the percentages as a rough guide.

1. Three estimates for every district, each a margin (Democrat minus Republican):

• Polls: the estimate in the Polls view, the generic-ballot average plus the district's lean. Districts are rarely polled on their own.
• Markets: what traders on Polymarket and Kalshi are paying, averaged when both list the district ( of 435 have a market with at least $ traded). A price of 70¢ means about a 70% chance; the model converts that into the margin that would give a 70% chance under its own uncertainty.
• Fundamentals: what history says without polls. In midterms since 1994 the president's party has run about 4.5 points behind in the House vote, plus about 1 point for every 6 points of net approval. Each district adds its partisan lean (2 × Cook PVI), and an incumbent running again gets points, since the lean alone ignores candidates.

2. A weighted blend. Polls count more as Election Day nears: their weight is 1 ÷ (1 + days left ÷ ). The rest goes 60% to markets and 40% to fundamentals. Today, days out: . Where a district has no market, its share goes to the others.

3. Uncertainty. The result will miss the blend. The model assumes a national miss shared by every district (typically about points), a regional miss shared by nearby states (about ), and a miss in each district on its own (about , larger than for states since district estimates are rougher). Big misses are drawn a little more often than a bell curve would.

4. Simulate. The model runs the election times with random misses. A district's chances are the share of runs each party wins it; Democrats control the House in runs where they win 218 or more seats.
