How this estimate works

There are almost no 2028 state polls yet, only national ones. So each state starts from a baseline: its 2024 result, shifted by however much the national polls have moved since 2024.

If a state has its own polls, they're blended with that baseline. Each state poll is first trend-adjusted by how much the national average has moved since it was taken. The baseline counts as fresh polls, so a few recent state polls quickly outweigh it.

Polls are weighted by recency (weight halves every days), sample size, and how many polls that pollster has released. Polls older than days are dropped. See every poll and its weight on the polls page.

How the forecast works

This is an unvalidated hobby model. It uses a standard method, but its settings are judgment calls, and it hasn't been tested against past elections the way professional forecasts are. Two years out, nobody can forecast a presidential race well: treat these percentages as a very rough guide.

1. Three estimates for every state, each a margin (Democrat minus Republican):

• Polls: the estimate in the Polls view (the 2024 result shifted by the national polls, plus any state polls). This is the only ingredient that depends on the candidates.
• Markets: what traders on Polymarket and Kalshi say about which party wins, averaged: . Neither lists individual states yet, so the model finds the national margin that would give that chance under its own uncertainty () and adds each state's lean (how far it was from the national result in 2024).
• Fundamentals: what history says without polls, loosely following Alan Abramowitz's "time for change" model. With no incumbent running, the president's party gains about 0.2 points of margin per point of net approval and loses about 2 points for each point unemployment rose over the past year. 2. A weighted blend. Polls count more as Election Day nears: their weight is 1 ÷ (1 + days left ÷ ). The rest goes 60% to markets and 40% to fundamentals. Today, days out: . That's why every matchup gets similar odds for now: the polls that tell them apart barely count yet.

3. Uncertainty. The result will miss the blend, by a lot this far out. The model assumes a national miss shared by every state (typically about points), a regional miss shared by nearby states (about ), and a miss in each state on its own (about ). These shrink as the election nears.

4. Simulate. The model runs the election times with random misses and counts electoral votes. A candidate's chance is the share of runs in which they reach 270. A 269–269 tie ( of runs) would go to the House and counts for neither. Maine and Nebraska's district electoral votes aren't modeled separately: each state's votes all go to its statewide winner.
