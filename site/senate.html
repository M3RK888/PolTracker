About these results

Results come from Wikipedia's 2026 Senate elections article, read once a day, and update as races are called. A race shows as uncalled until a winner is marked there. The Forecast view still shows the forecast exactly as it stood on Election Day morning, and the accuracy page grades it race by race.

How this estimate works

Each race starts from a baseline: the state's partisan lean (Cook PVI) applied to the national environment. The environment is the average of the major poll aggregators' generic congressional ballot averages, currently .

The race's own polls are then blended in. Poll weight halves every days, rises with sample size, and is reduced when one pollster has several polls of the race. The baseline counts as fresh polls, so in a well-polled race the polls decide the estimate almost entirely.

Independents. Where the main challenger is an independent (Nebraska, Idaho, Montana, South Dakota), the estimate is that independent against the Republican, and a win counts on the Democrats' side of the seat count. They haven't all said who they'd caucus with.

Control. 65 seats aren't up this year (34 Democratic-caucus, 31 Republican). Democrats need 51 seats for a majority; Republicans need 50, since Vice President Vance breaks ties. Ratings use the same cutoffs as the 2028 map. This view is a snapshot of the polls; switch to Forecast for chances of winning.

How the forecast works

This is an unvalidated hobby model. It uses a standard method, but its settings are judgment calls, and it hasn't been tested against past elections the way professional forecasts are. Treat the percentages as a rough guide.

1. Three estimates for every race, each a margin (Democrat minus Republican):

• Polls: the estimate in the Polls view (the race's polls blended with the generic ballot).
• Markets: what traders on Polymarket and Kalshi are paying, averaged when both list the race. A price of 70¢ means about a 70% chance; the model converts that into the margin that would give a 70% chance under its own uncertainty. Markets with less than $ traded are ignored.
• Fundamentals: what history says without polls. In midterms since 1994 the president's party has run about 4.5 points behind in the House vote, plus about 1 point for every 6 points of net approval. Trump's net approval is , which points to a national environment of (the generic-ballot polls say ). Each state adds its partisan lean (2 × Cook PVI), and an incumbent on the ballot gets points.

2. A weighted blend. Polls count more as Election Day nears: their weight is 1 ÷ (1 + days left ÷ ). The rest goes 60% to markets and 40% to fundamentals. Today, days out: . If a race has no market, its share goes to the others.

3. Uncertainty. The result will miss the blend. The model assumes a national miss shared by every race (typically about points), a regional miss shared by nearby states (about ), and a miss in each race on its own (about ). These grow the further out the election is. Big misses are drawn a little more often than a bell curve would.

4. Simulate. The model runs the election times with random misses. A candidate's chance is the share of runs they win; Democrats control the Senate in runs where they (with independents) reach 51 seats, counting the 34 not up. Independents running against a Republican count on the Democratic side.
